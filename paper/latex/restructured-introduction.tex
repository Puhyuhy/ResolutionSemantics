\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

A partial algebra is an algebra in which some applications of the basic
operations are left undefined.  There are several classical ways of replacing
a partial algebra by a total one.  In this paper we use the free compatible
completion only as a convenient ambient algebra: undefined applications are
retained as formal terms, while applications already defined in the original
algebra are reduced to their prescribed values.  This construction is standard
and is not part of the novelty claim; see, for example, the literature on
Schmidt-type completions and partial-to-total encodings
\cite{ClimentVidalCosmeLlopez2023,Diaconescu2009}.

Our question is more specific.  Let $D$ be a one-sorted binary partial algebra
with carrier $A$.  We ask how well elements of its free compatible completion
can be distinguished by total extensions that preserve the whole base $A$ but
add only finitely many new elements.

More precisely, a \emph{finite-complement extension} of $D$ will mean a
compatible total algebra $T$ equipped with an injective map $i:A\to T$ such
that $T\setminus i(A)$ is finite.  We identify $A$ with its image and therefore
speak of extensions that preserve the base pointwise.  Notice that $T$ need
not be finite: when $A$ is infinite, the restriction is only on the number of
new elements.

The main theorem is the following finite-family separation statement.

\begin{theorem}[Finite-family separation; informal form]
\label{thm:intro-finite-family}
For every finite set $F$ of elements of the free compatible completion of $D$,
there exists a finite-complement extension $T$ of $D$ such that the canonical
homomorphism into $T$ is injective on $F$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is direct.  One takes the finite set of subterms occurring in the
chosen elements, assigns distinct new states to the unresolved subterms that
must be remembered, and sends all remaining unresolved behaviour to one
additional sink state.  The normalization of the free completion ensures that
this finite table is compatible with every operation already defined in the
base algebra.

For a finite base, a result of this form is close to ordinary finite
separation.  The point here is that the base may be infinite and is never
quotiented or collapsed.  Only the complement is finite.  This distinction is
not cosmetic.  The class of such extensions is not closed under ordinary
binary products when the base is infinite: even the diagonal copy of
$\mathbb N$ in
\[
 (\mathbb N\sqcup\{\star\})^2
\]
has infinitely many points outside it.  Thus the standard argument that
combines pairwise separators by taking a finite product does not remain inside
the same class.  The finite-family theorem is therefore obtained by a direct
construction.

The finite-complement extensions induce a descending family of equivalence
relations on the free completion: two elements are equivalent at budget $n$ if
no compatible extension using at most $n+1$ new states distinguishes them.  The
finite-family theorem implies that the intersection of these relations is
equality.  We then consider the Cauchy completion of this separated filtered
space.

A second part of the paper concerns when the resulting completion is proper,
that is, when it contains points not represented by finite terms.  Two cases
are treated.

First, when the base is finitely coded, we obtain an exact criterion:
\[
 \text{the completion is proper}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \text{the original evaluator is not total}.
\]
Thus every genuinely partial finite-base algebra gives rise to new completion
points.

Second, for arbitrary bases we give a sufficient criterion.  Suppose an
undefined application produces a non-base term and there is a unary context
that acts as the identity on every base element.  Iterating that context yields
a sequence whose suitable factorial subsequence is Cauchy at every finite
budget, while no finite generated term can be its limit.  This proves
properness without any finiteness assumption on the base.  Natural and integer
arithmetic with division undefined at zero provide simple examples, using the
context $x\mapsto x+0$.

The proofs of the completion statements use only elementary finite-state
periodicity, factorial divisibility, and the same finite-subterm construction
used in the separation theorem.  We isolate these ingredients because they make
the argument transparent, but we do not claim novelty for the finite-state
machinery itself.

The relation with earlier work is as follows.  Free completion of partial
algebras, tree recognition, residual separation, profinite completion, and the
many-sorted encoding of partial algebras are established theories.  The narrow
contribution considered here is the finite-complement separation problem under
pointwise preservation of a possibly infinite partial base, together with the
properness consequences derived from that observer class.  The exact novelty
and priority of this formulation remain matters for specialist evaluation.

The paper is organized as follows.  \Cref{sec:free-completion} records the free
compatible completion used throughout.  \Cref{sec:finite-separation} proves the
finite-family separation theorem and the product obstruction.
\Cref{sec:completion} constructs the filtered completion and proves the
finite-base and arbitrary-base properness results.  \Cref{sec:equations} treats
universal equations, and \cref{sec:arithmetic} gives the arithmetic examples.
The many-sorted comparison with Diaconescu's encoding is kept separate in
\cref{sec:diaconescu-bridge}; it is included for orientation, not as a separate
novelty claim.  The final sections compare the construction with related work
and state its limitations.

All mathematical arguments are presented independently of the accompanying
formalization.  The Lean sources are supplementary verification and are
summarized only in the appendix.
